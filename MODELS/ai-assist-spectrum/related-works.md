# Related Work

The AI‑Assist Spectrum (AS) exists within a broader landscape of frameworks concerned with authorship, transparency, and human–AI collaboration. While several systems address adjacent concerns, none provide a hierarchical, epistemic model of contribution comparable to the Spectrum. The most relevant categories of related work are summarized below.

---

**Preface**

This document was created after the initial publication of the AI‑Assist Spectrum.  
Its purpose is to help readers understand how the Spectrum relates to other frameworks that address authorship, transparency, or AI involvement.  
It is descriptive rather than evaluative, and it does not imply that prior research informed the original development of the Spectrum.

## 1. Authorship Transparency Frameworks

### Authorship Transparent Statement (ATS) Framework
A structured disclosure template that documents which tools were used, how they were used, and which parts of a work involved AI assistance. ATS emphasizes procedural transparency, ethical clarity, and reader trust.

**Relation to AS:**  
- Complementary, not overlapping  
- ATS is a *form*; AS is a *model*  
- ATS documents *what happened*; AS classifies *what it means*

### Artificial Intelligence Disclosure (AID) Framework
A multi‑field academic disclosure system that records AI involvement across conceptualization, methodology, analysis, and writing.

**Relation to AS:**  
- Similar transparency goals  
- No authorship hierarchy  
- No epistemic grounding  
- Procedural rather than conceptual

### IAB AI Transparency and Disclosure Framework
Used in advertising to determine when AI‑generated content requires disclosure.

**Relation to AS:**  
- Focuses on consumer transparency  
- Not concerned with authorship or contribution semantics

## 2. Academic Authorship Models

### Five‑Step Authorship Framework (Clinical Research)
Defines human authorship responsibilities such as conception, design, analysis, drafting, and approval.

**Relation to AS:**  
- Addresses human contribution  
- Not AI‑specific  
- No generative workflow modeling  
- No epistemic categories

### Journal Authorship Policies
Institutional guidelines that define authorship credit and accountability.

**Relation to AS:**  
- Concerned with responsibility  
- Not concerned with AI contribution levels  
- No hierarchical model

---

## 3. Conceptual Models of AI Authorship

### AI as Tool / Co‑Author / Creator Models
A common three‑category framing found in academic and legal discussions.

**Relation to AS:**  
- Attempts to classify AI’s role  
- Too coarse for real workflows  
- Lacks epistemic grounding  
- No evaluation method  
- No disclosure language

### Legal and Copyright Analyses
Scholarship on whether AI‑generated works can be copyrighted and how human authorship is defined.

**Relation to AS:**  
- Addresses boundaries of authorship  
- Not operational  
- No workflow semantics  
- No hierarchy or contribution levels

---

## 4. AI Governance and Risk Frameworks

### Responsible AI Guidelines
Focus on fairness, bias, safety, and accountability.

**Relation to AS:**  
- Concerned with system‑level ethics  
- Not concerned with authorship or contribution semantics

### Model Cards / System Cards
Documentation for AI systems describing capabilities, limitations, and intended use.

**Relation to AS:**  
- Transparency‑oriented  
- Not about human–AI co‑authorship  
- No artifact‑level evaluation

# Positioning Statement

The AI‑Assist Spectrum (AS) occupies a distinct space among frameworks concerned with authorship and AI involvement. Existing systems tend to focus on transparency, disclosure, or ethical compliance, while the Spectrum provides a structured model for understanding the nature of human–AI contribution itself.

The Spectrum differs from disclosure templates such as the Authorship Transparent Statement (ATS) Framework and the Artificial Intelligence Disclosure (AID) Framework, which document *what tools were used* and *how they were used*. These systems are procedural and form‑based. The Spectrum is conceptual: it defines levels of authorship contribution ranging from light refinement to full co‑development and invention.

The Spectrum also differs from academic authorship guidelines and legal analyses, which address responsibility, credit, and copyright boundaries but do not model AI involvement at varying degrees of creative or analytical labor.

In contrast to coarse conceptual models that categorize AI as a tool, co‑author, or creator, the Spectrum offers a more granular hierarchy that reflects real creative workflows and provides evaluators with a consistent method for classifying outputs.

In summary, the AI‑Assist Spectrum is the only framework that provides a clear, level‑based model of authorship contribution in human–AI collaboration. It complements existing transparency and disclosure systems while offering a conceptual structure they do not attempt to provide.
