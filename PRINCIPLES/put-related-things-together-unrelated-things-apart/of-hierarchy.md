# Of Hierarchy
<p align="right">Refined with assistance of AI tools</p

In a hierarchical structure, this principle maps onto two axes.

**Horizontal (breadth):** Siblings share a parent — that shared parent is the signal of relatedness. Items under different parents are separated by that divergence; their distinction is structurally encoded.

**Vertical (depth/path):** The path from root to a node is its lineage. Two nodes are related in proportion to how much of that path they share. The longer the shared prefix, the closer the relationship. The earlier the divergence, the more fundamental the distinction. The lowest common ancestor (LCA) is the exact point where relatedness ends and unrelatedness begins.

The principle holds on both axes. A well-designed hierarchy places things that share a deep LCA as siblings — and keeps things with a shallow LCA far apart in depth. When a hierarchy violates this — placing things with a shallow LCA as siblings — it creates accidental coupling. Separating things with a deep LCA under different branches scatters what belongs together.

Hierarchy is one of the few structures where relatedness is encoded spatially and structurally at the same time. The principle doesn't just apply to hierarchy — hierarchy is a machine for expressing it. When a hierarchy is well-designed, the structure *is* the statement of what's related and what isn't.
