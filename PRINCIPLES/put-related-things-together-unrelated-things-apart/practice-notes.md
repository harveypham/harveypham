## Define the Concept First
The principle does not define "related" or "unrelated" — that is the practitioner's job. Before applying the principle, define the concept that determines relatedness for the task at hand. The grouping follows from that definition; the principle tells you what to do once you've decided.

## Choosing Boundaries
The core practice is to choose boundaries to reflect your concept. Every medium has natural boundary types, each enforcing a different level of isolation:

- **Code:** blank lines, functions, files, directories, repositories
- **Writing:** sentences, paragraphs, sections, chapters

Choose the boundary type whose isolation strength matches the conceptual separation you want to express. Mismatching — using a weak boundary where a strong one is warranted, or splitting what belongs together — misleads the reader about the relationship.

### When Natural Boundaries Are Exhausted
Natural boundary types are finite. When one natural boundary must represent more than one conceptual boundary type, the mapping becomes ambiguous. Restore stability with virtual constructs: naming conventions, prefixes, section markers, or other signals that identify which conceptual boundary a natural structure represents.

The cost: natural boundaries enforce themselves; virtual constructs require the practitioner to enforce them through self-discipline.

### Prefer Natural Over Virtual
When possible, favor natural boundaries over virtual ones. Natural boundaries shift enforcement from the practitioner to the medium. Part of sharpening boundaries over time is finding opportunities to promote a stable virtual boundary into a natural one.

## Apply Iteratively
The principle can be applied in passes. Each iteration sharpens the boundaries — removing ambiguities, exposing hidden relationships, and refining the concept definition itself. Iteration is not just correction — it reflects a deeper understanding of the relationship.
